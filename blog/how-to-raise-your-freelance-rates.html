How to Raise Your Freelance Rates (Without Losing Clients)

Raising rates feels risky, so most freelancers don't — and quietly work below market for years. The data point that changes minds: clients who value your work rarely leave over a professional, well-communicated increase. The ones who leave were usually the problem clients anyway.

The announcement structure that works

  1. Lead with the relationship — one genuine line about the work together.
  2. State the new rate plainly — no apology, no essay. One sentence.
  3. Give the effective date — 30 days out is standard and fair.
  4. Grandfather selectively — offer long-tenured clients a 3-month bridge rate. Generosity with a deadline.
  5. Re-state what they get — outcomes delivered, not hours logged.

Timing rules

Never announce mid-project. Announce at renewal, at project close, or at the new year. Pair the increase with a capability note ("I now include X") so the change reads as a service change, not a squeeze.

What to do about the client who pushes back

One polite hold: "I keep my rates fair and my quality high — here's what the new rate includes." If they still walk, the audit you ran before raising (hours vs. rate per client) usually reveals they were your worst margin anyway.

FAQ

How much of an increase is acceptable? 10–20% annually is unremarkable for good freelancers in 2026; under-market rates can justify more with the bridge-rate approach.

Email or call? Email for the written record, call your top two or three clients personally first.
