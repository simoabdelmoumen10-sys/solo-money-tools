How to Price House Cleaning Jobs in 2026 (Rate Sheet Formula)

Most new residential cleaners lose money before they ever spray a bottle — because they price by gut feel. The cleaners who grow price with a formula: base rate per room type, condition multiplier, and a written add-on menu. Here is the structure, and a kit that fills it in for you.

1. Set a base rate per service type

Standard clean, deep clean, and move-out clean are three different products. A standard clean prices per home size (bedroom count bands); deep cleans price at 1.5–2× standard; move-outs price flat by square footage because empty homes are a different job entirely.

2. Apply a condition multiplier

First cleans are almost always heavier than maintenance cleans. Price the first visit at your deep-clean rate, then quote the recurring rate separately — clients accept this when you explain "first clean sets the baseline."

3. Publish an add-on menu

Inside-the-fridge, inside-the-oven, interior windows, laundry switch: fixed prices, on your price sheet, quoted before you arrive. Add-ons are where ticket size grows without new clients.

4. Quote with a script, not a guess

Phone quotes convert when you ask the same five questions every time (beds, baths, pets, last professional clean, access). A written script keeps quotes consistent and protects your margin.

FAQ

How much should a standard clean cost? Market-dependent, but solo operators typically land between $80 and $150 for a standard clean of a 2–3 bedroom home in US metros in 2026.

Should I charge hourly or flat? Flat per-job pricing protects you as you get faster; hourly exposes you to slow first cleans. Quote flat, estimate internally by hour.

How do I price a first deep clean? 1.5–2× your standard rate, quoted as a one-time "baseline clean," then drop to the recurring rate.
